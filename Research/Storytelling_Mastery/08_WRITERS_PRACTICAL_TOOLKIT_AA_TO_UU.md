## 1. Point of View (POV): Choosing Your Camera

The most common mistake of first-time writers is **Head-Hopping** (jumping randomly from one character's thoughts to another's in the same scene). This confuses the reader and destroys immersion.

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                        THE THREE CORE POV MODES                        │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 1. FIRST PERSON ("I felt the heat...")                                 │
│    • Hyper-intimate, conversational, limited strictly to one mind.     │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 2. THIRD PERSON OMNISCIENT ("The town was silent, and both knew it...")│
│    • God-like narrator, sees past, present, and all minds at once.    │
│    • Can feel distant, cold, and journalistic if not handled expertly. │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 3. THIRD PERSON LIMITED (DEEP POV) - THE MODERN INDUSTRY STANDARD     │
│    • Narrated in third person ("He/She"), but the camera is welded     │
│      inside ONE character's skull for the entire scene.                │
│    • Every smell, word choice, and observation reflects THEIR bias.   │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### The Deep POV Commandments:
1. **One POV Per Scene:** If Chapter 1 is from Vikram’s perspective, the reader ONLY knows what Vikram can see, hear, smell, and deduce.
2. **Never Report Another Character's Internal State:**
   - *Amateur / Head-Hopping:* "Vikram drew his blade. Maya felt terrified that he would die." *(How does Vikram know Maya is terrified? He can't read her mind!)*
   - *Professional Deep POV:* "Vikram drew his blade. Behind him, Maya’s breath hitched, her fingers clutching his coat so hard the seams strained." *(Vikram observes the physical evidence and deduces her fear).*

## 2. Show, Don’t Tell (The Concrete Sensory Palette)

Telling reports abstract conclusions. Showing delivers raw sensory data so the **reader’s own brain draws the conclusion**.

### The Before & After Master Matrix:

| Telling (Flat, Weak, Boring) | Showing (Vivid, Sensory, Cinematic) | What Changed? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| *Karna was exhausted from the shift.* | *Karna’s knees shook as he stepped off the iron footplate. Coal dust was caked so thick in the creases of his neck that sweat carved grey rivers down his throat.* | Replaced the generic adjective "exhausted" with physical weight, tremble, and sweat. |
| *The tea shop was cheap and filthy.* | *A cloudy glass jar of stale cardamom biscuits sat beside a puddle of spilled kerosene. Flies buzzed over the dented tin counter.* | Specified exact objects, smells, and textures. |
| *Inspector Rao was an angry, impatient man.* | *Rao didn't wait for the clerk to open the ledger. He drove his swagger stick through the center of the desk, shattering the ceramic inkwell.* | Replaced description with an immediate violent physical action. |

### The Five-Sense Check (In Every Scene):
- **Smell:** Hot sulfur, burnt engine oil, wet wool, stale tobacco, boiling mustard seed.
- **Sound:** The rhythmic clanking of rail car couplings, the wet slap of boots on mud, the faint wheeze of a leaking valve.
- **Touch / Texture:** Gritty brick dust on fingertips, the bite of cold iron through thin gloves, the stiff collar chafing raw skin.
- **Taste:** Bitter black tea stewed too long, the metallic copper tang of blood in the mouth, dry grit between the molars.
- **Sight:** Not just "a building", but "a three-story brick tenement with rotting timber balconies and soot-stained laundry hanging on telephone wire."

## 3. Dialogue Mastery: Making Speech Crackle

Dialogue in fiction is **not real-life speech**. Real-life speech is full of "uhms", small talk, and boring greetings. Fiction dialogue is **condensed verbal combat**.

### 3.1 The "As You Know, Bob" Trap (Exposition Dumping)
- **The Sin:** Having two characters tell each other things they both already know just to explain lore to the reader.
  - *Terrible:* *"As you know, Inspector, our city was split into five astral dimensions forty years ago after the Ashvattha tree cracked."*
  - *Fix:* Eliminate the speech. Have them argue about something immediate: *"If we don't catch the 06:15 train to the Central Shard, the border seal locks, and we rot in this slum for another month."*

### 3.2 Action Beats Over Dialogue Tags
Minimize the use of dialogue tags (`said`, `whispered`, `shouted`, `exclaimed`). Replace them with **Action Beats** that reveal emotion and stage the physical scene:

- *Weak:*  
  "I don't have the silver," Arjun said anxiously.  
  "You'd better find it," Dev replied menacingly.

- *Strong:*  
  Arjun turned his pockets inside out, letting two bent copper mashas clatter against the counter. "I don't have the silver."  
  Dev didn't blink. He slid a four-inch carving knife from his sleeve, testing the edge against his thumb until a bead of dark blood welled up. "You'd better find it."

## 4. Pacing Control: Sentence Geometry & The White Space Rule

You control the reader's adrenaline entirely through **punctuation and line breaks**:

### 4.1 Fast Pacing (Fights, Explosions, Escapes, Panic)
- Short sentences.
- Active, physical verbs.
- Frequent paragraph breaks.
- Eliminate adverbs and explanatory clauses.

> *"The boiler groaned. Pressure spiked. The gauge needle snapped. Run."*

### 4.2 Slow Pacing (Mystery, Dread, Reflection, Grief)
- Long, flowing, multi-clause sentences.
- Slower rhythm, sensory texture, atmospheric descriptions.
- Larger paragraphs that demand slow, contemplative reading.

> *"In the dim candlelight of the lower archive, where stacks of forgotten railway manifests smelled of mildew and decaying glue, the silence was broken only by the distant, muffled thrum of the harbor pumps three streets away."*

## 5. The 3-Draft Workflow: From Blank Page to Masterpiece

Never try to write a perfect novel on your first try. Nobody does. Great books are not written; **they are rewritten**.

```
  DRAFT 1: THE PUKE DRAFT ──► DRAFT 2: STRUCTURAL SURGERY ──► DRAFT 3: LINE POLISH
  (Discovery / Speed)         (Plot, Character Arcs, Logic)   (Word Choice & Rhythm)
```

### Draft 1: The Discovery Draft ("Telling Yourself the Story")
- **Rule:** Write with relentless momentum. Turn off your inner critic.
- **Do not edit yesterday's words.** If you realize in Chapter 12 that a character should have died in Chapter 3, write a quick bracketed note `[NOTE: Fix in Draft 2: Kill Pandu in Ch 3]` and keep writing forward!
- The goal of Draft 1 is simply to make the story exist.

### Draft 2: The Structural Overhaul
- Now put on your Architect hat.
- Check the 3-Act / Save the Cat milestones: Is the midpoint strong? Did the hero earn the climax victory?
- Check side characters: Are they acting according to their own 5-point mindsets?
- Fix plot holes, timeline inconsistencies, and power system contradictions.

### Draft 3: The Line Polish & Word-Diet (The 10% Cut)
- Read your prose out loud. Your ear will instantly catch clunky sentences that your eyes missed.
- **The 10% Cut Rule (Stephen King):** Cut 10% of the total word count.
  - Delete weak filler words: *very, really, suddenly, seemed to, began to, felt like*.
  - Change passive voice to active voice (*"The door was kicked open by Vikram"* ➔ *"Vikram kicked the door off its hinges"*).
  - Verify every chapter ends on a compelling question or cliffhanger.
